How to Read Your Small Business Financial Statements and Cash Flow

Here's something that confuses more business owners than almost anything else in their financials: you can be profitable on paper and still not have enough cash to make payroll. Those are two different questions, "am I profitable" and "do I have cash," and they need two different statements to answer, not one.

Published September 2nd, 2026 | By Stephen Daperis, CPA

The P&L: Are You Actually Making Money?

Your P&L (also called an income statement) shows revenue minus expenses over a period, monthly, quarterly, annually, landing on your net profit or loss. What should you actually look at each month? Not just the bottom line. Look at the trend underneath it: is revenue growing or flat, is a specific expense category creeping up faster than revenue, is your gross margin, revenue minus the direct cost of delivering your product or service, holding steady or slowly shrinking.

A shrinking gross margin is one of the earliest real warning signs in a business, and it's easy to miss if you're only glancing at the final profit number instead of what's happening above it.

The Balance Sheet: What You Own, What You Owe

The balance sheet is a snapshot at a single point in time: what the business owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and the difference between the two (equity). Most owners skip this one entirely and only look at the P&L, which means they miss real problems, accounts receivable quietly growing because customers are taking longer to pay, or debt that's crept up without anyone tracking it against the business's actual ability to service it.

The Cash Flow Statement: Where the Confusion Actually Lives

This is the one that resolves the "profitable but broke" problem. It tracks actual cash moving in and out, which is different from the P&L, because the P&L includes non-cash items like depreciation, and it can show revenue you've earned but haven't collected yet, or expenses you've incurred but haven't paid yet.

So how does a business show a healthy profit on the P&L while cash flow runs negative? Usually one of three ways: customers are paying slowly and accounts receivable is piling up, inventory is tying up cash, or a big loan payment or piece of equipment got paid for in cash but is only being expensed gradually over time. This is, by far, the most common reason a profitable-looking business still struggles to make payroll or pay vendors on time. It's also the reason "but the P&L says we're fine" is one of the more dangerous sentences in small business.

What Should You Actually Check Every Month?

You don't need to read all three statements line by line every month. What matters is a short, consistent routine: check your cash balance and how many weeks of expenses it covers, check your gross margin trend on the P&L, check how much is sitting in accounts receivable and how old the oldest balances are, and check whether any expense category jumped without an obvious reason.

Red Flags Worth Acting On

A few patterns are worth treating as real signals, not noise: gross margin declining for more than a month or two in a row, accounts receivable that keeps growing month over month (collections are slipping), cash balance shrinking even though the P&L shows a profit, and any single expense category growing noticeably faster than revenue.

None of these are emergencies the first time you see them. They're a pattern worth investigating if they show up two or three months running.

Profitable vs. Cash-Flow Positive, the Actual Difference

Profitable means your revenue exceeded your expenses over a period, on paper. Cash-flow positive means more cash actually came in than went out. A business can be one without the other, in either direction, and if you're only checking the P&L, you're flying with half the instrument panel dark.

Keep It Simple / Key Takeaway 🍕

Think of the P&L as your speedometer, it tells you how fast you're going. The cash flow statement is your fuel gauge, it tells you whether you're actually going to make it. You need both. A business can look like it's flying and still be running on fumes if nobody's watching the fuel gauge.
