Loan-Out Companies in NYC: When They Pay Off, and the City Tax Nobody Mentions

Ask around any rehearsal room, set, or edit bay and someone will tell you to get a loan-out. Their accountant set one up, their agent recommended it, and everybody in Los Angeles has one. It's easy, right? Run your gigs through your own corporation, write everything off and then save a fortune on taxes.

Half of that is (sort of) right. In New York City, the other half can cost you money.

Published November 11th, 2026 | By Stephen Daperis, CPA

What a loan-out actually is

A loan-out is a corporation you own whose real business is renting out your services. A production hires your company instead of you, whether you're an actor, a session musician, or a cinematographer. Your company gets paid, pays you a salary through payroll, and whatever's left can come out to you as a distribution.

It's almost always taxed as an S corporation, so the profit flows through to your personal return instead of being taxed twice.

That's the whole structure. The tax value comes entirely from what it changes about how you get paid.

Why W-2 actors, musicians, and crew are stuck without one

When a production pays you directly as an employee, you're a W-2 worker. And W-2 workers have nowhere to deduct the cost of working.

Unreimbursed employee expenses used to be a miscellaneous itemized deduction. IRC §67(g) suspended those deductions starting in 2018, and everyone expected them back in 2026. Instead, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the suspension permanent.

In plain English: as a W-2 performer, your agent's commission, your manager's cut, your attorney's percentage, union dues, coaching, headshots, self-tapes, and audition travel are deductible at exactly zero. Commissions alone can run 20% or more of your gross, and you pay income tax on that money as if you kept it.

The same goes for a session or touring musician paying for instruments, rehearsal space, and gig travel out of pocket, or a camera operator or editor on a production's payroll who owns their own gear. If the paycheck is a W-2, the cost of earning it isn't deductible.

There is a special break for "qualified performing artists" under §62(a)(2)(B) that moves these expenses above the line. The catch is that your adjusted gross income can't exceed $16,000. Congress set that number in 1986 and never indexed it. If you earn enough to have a manager, you don't qualify.

What the loan-out fixes

When the production pays your corporation, your commissions and professional costs become business expenses of the company, deducted before any money reaches you. For most performers, that alone is the biggest win. For a musician, that includes management and booking commissions, instruments, and rehearsal space. For a video producer or cinematographer, it's the camera package, software, and assistants.

The secondary benefits:

  • Retirement savings at a real scale. Your corporation can sponsor a solo 401(k). You contribute as the employee, and the company can add up to 25% of your W-2 salary on top.
  • Health insurance. Premiums the S corporation pays for a more-than-2% shareholder get added to your W-2 and deducted on your personal return, generally the same deduction a self-employed person gets.
  • Payroll tax on distributions. You pay yourself a reasonable salary, and profit above that can come out as a distribution without Social Security or Medicare tax.

That last bullet is the one people lead with. In New York City, it's the one to be most skeptical of.

The NYC catch: General Corporation Tax

New York City does not recognize the S election. Your loan-out can be an S corporation for federal purposes, and for New York State if you elect it there too, but the city taxes it under the General Corporation Tax as if no election existed.

The GCT rate is 8.85% of the company's income allocated to the city. There's also a fixed-dollar minimum, which you owe even in a year the company loses money. Smaller NYC-only corporations compute the tax on net income alone. Larger or more complex ones also have to test an alternative base that adds back part of the owner's salary, which can produce city tax even when every dollar goes out as wages.

New York State has its own wrinkle. The state S election isn't automatic. You file Form CT-6, or New York taxes your company as a C corporation at the state level, on top of everything else.

Here's what that changes. NYC only taxes the profit your company keeps after paying your salary, and distributions come out of exactly that profit. So in NYC, the real question isn't whether to have a loan-out. It's how to split your pay between salary and distributions.

How that split works (rough math, before federal deductions):

  • Below the Social Security wage base ($184,500 for 2026), a dollar taken as a distribution instead of salary saves about 15.3% in payroll tax and costs 8.85% in city tax. Distributions still come out ahead, just by a smaller margin than most assume.
  • Above the wage base, a distribution only saves Medicare tax (2.9%, or 3.8% once the Additional Medicare Tax applies), which is less than the 8.85% city tax. Here, salary is the cheaper way to pay yourself, and a higher salary also raises what the company can put into your solo 401(k).

Either way, the rest of the loan-out's value stays intact. You get the business deductions you couldn't take as a W-2 performer, access to a company retirement plan, and possibly a QBI deduction on profit that isn't paid out as salary (performers fall in a specialized-service category, so that one phases out at higher incomes). NYC changes how you split your pay, not whether the loan-out is worth having.

As a W-2 performer, you never paid a city business tax. With a loan-out, you do. That doesn't make it a bad idea, but it makes it a cost you model up front instead of discovering on your first city return.

Who should actually have one

A loan-out is worth modeling if:

  • Most of your income is union or production work (acting, session and touring gigs, crew days) you can route through the company
  • Commissions and professional costs are a meaningful share of your gross
  • You want to put serious money into retirement
  • The productions you work with will pay your company instead of you. Many will, but some won't for various reasons. You're going to want to confirm before you form anything.

Skip it if:

  • You book occasional day-player or background work. Payroll, filings, and minimum taxes will eat the benefit.
  • Your income is mostly 1099 freelance work. Many video producers, editors, and independent musicians are paid this way. Those costs are already deductible on Schedule C, and your real question is whether an S-corp election pays off, which is a different analysis.
  • You won't keep the company's money and books separate from your own. The structure only holds up if it's run like a real company.

Before you form it

  • Run the numbers with NYC in them. GCT, the city and state fixed-dollar minimums, and payroll costs. A template built for another state doesn't include any of that.
  • File both elections on time. Form 2553 for the federal S election and Form CT-6 for New York State.
  • Set a reasonable salary before the first distribution. The IRS expects an S-corp owner who works in the business to be paid wages that reflect the work.
  • Expect out-of-state paperwork. California withholds tax on payments for services performed there, including payments to loan-outs, and other states have their own rules. Working out of town usually means a nonresident return.
  • Confirm union terms. Union contracts still govern covered work. Check with your union how pension and health contributions are handled when your company signs the deal.

The verdict

In New York City, a loan-out is a good tool sold with the wrong headline. Its real value is moving your commissions and costs off a W-2, where they're worthless, and into a company that can deduct them, plus a retirement plan with real limits.

Common questions

Does New York City recognize S corporations?

No. NYC taxes S corporations under the General Corporation Tax at 8.85% of city-allocated income, as if the S election didn't exist.

Can I deduct my agent's commission as a W-2 actor?

Not on your federal return, unless you meet the qualified performing artist test, which requires AGI of $16,000 or less. Unreimbursed employee expenses are permanently non-deductible after the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

Does a loan-out still save payroll tax in NYC?

Below the Social Security wage base, usually yes, though the 8.85% city tax eats a large share of it. Above the wage base, extra distributions can cost more in city tax than they save in Medicare tax.

Is an LLC the same as a loan-out?

An LLC can serve as a loan-out if it elects S corporation treatment, and NYC then taxes it under the GCT the same way. A single-member LLC without the election is disregarded, so it doesn't change how a production pays you.

Do I need New York's CT-6 if I already filed Form 2553?

Yes. The federal election doesn't carry over. Without CT-6, New York State taxes your company as a C corporation.

Does a loan-out make sense for a musician or video producer?

It can. A session or touring musician paid on a W-2, or a cinematographer or editor put on a production's payroll, faces the same non-deductible costs as an actor. If you're paid on a 1099 and invoice clients directly, you can already deduct those costs on Schedule C, and the real question is whether an S-corp election pays off.
