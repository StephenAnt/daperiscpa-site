LLC vs. S Corp: Which Structure Actually Saves You Money

"Should I be an LLC or an S corp?" is the wrong question, and I hear it every week.

An LLC is a legal structure. An S corp is a tax election. They're not the same kind of thing, which is exactly why the question trips people up. A single-member LLC can choose to be taxed as an S corporation. What people usually mean is: should my business keep paying self-employment tax on everything it makes, or should I make this election to cut that down.

That's the real question, and it has a real answer. But it depends on your numbers, not a rule of thumb from a Google search.

Published August 25th, 2026 | By Stephen Daperis, CPA

How Sole Proprietors and Single-Member LLCs Get Taxed

If you run your business as a sole proprietor, or as a single-member LLC that hasn't made any special election, the IRS treats you the same way. All your profit flows to your personal return and gets hit with self-employment tax, 15.3% for Social Security and Medicare, on top of regular income tax. There's no split between "your salary" and "the business's profit." It's all one pot, taxed the same way.

For a profitable business, that tax adds up fast, on every dollar of profit, not just what you take home to live on.

What Changes With an S-Corp Election

An S corp splits your income into two buckets. You pay yourself a reasonable salary as an employee of your own business, and that salary gets the same 15.3%, split between you and the business. Whatever's left over after that salary comes out as a distribution, and distributions skip the self-employment tax entirely. They're still taxed as regular income, just without that extra hit.

That gap, the tax a sole proprietor pays on everything but an S-corp owner skips on distributions, is where the savings come from. It's real, it's legal, and it's exactly what this structure was built to do.

The Catch: Your Salary Has to Be Real

The IRS doesn't let you pay yourself nothing and take everything as a distribution. Your salary has to be "reasonable," meaning roughly what someone else would get paid to do your job, in your market. There's no official formula, no magic 40/60 split. If a firm tells you there's a fixed ratio, that's a rule of thumb, not the actual law, and the IRS has won cases against S-corp owners who paid themselves too little. Get caught, and the IRS can reclassify your distributions as wages after the fact, which means back taxes, penalties, and interest all at once.

Paying yourself too much is its own mistake too. Every extra dollar of salary above what's defensible costs you payroll tax for nothing, and it can shrink a separate deduction we'll get to below. Base the number on what you actually do, and keep records, don't pick a number to hit a refund target.

Where the Math Starts to Work in Your Favor

An S corp costs more to run than a plain LLC: real payroll, a separate corporate tax return, more bookkeeping. Below a certain profit level, those costs can eat up the whole tax savings, or more. Above that level, the savings usually win by a good margin. Exactly where that line sits depends on your profit, your salary number, and what payroll and filing actually cost you. Run your real numbers instead of guessing from something you read online.

The Deduction People Forget: QBI

There's another piece worth knowing about: the Qualified Business Income deduction. At lower and moderate income, this can be worth up to 20% of your business income, taken right on your personal return, no matter which structure you use.

At higher income, though, this deduction gets limited by how much W-2 wages the business pays. A sole proprietor with no wages at all can lose the whole deduction once income crosses a certain point. An S-corp owner, because they're already required to pay themselves a salary, often keeps some or all of that deduction at the same income level where a sole proprietor loses it. This can matter just as much as the self-employment tax savings for a high earner, and it's rarely part of the conversation.

The New York City Piece

If you're in New York City, there's a wrinkle you won't find in most general advice online. The city charges its own General Corporation Tax on S-corps operating there, on top of everything at the federal level, and separate from what applies to C-corps. That's a real cost that has to be part of the math for a NYC business, and most national calculators don't account for it.

There's also a separate yearly filing for LLCs in New York, called IT-204-LL, that applies whether or not you've elected S-corp treatment. It's different from your business's biennial statement, and it's easy to forget you owe two separate things.

So, Which One Should You Pick

If your business is new, your profit is modest, or you're still finding your footing, stay a simple LLC or sole proprietor and keep things easy.

Once your profit reaches a point where the tax savings clearly beat the added cost of payroll and filing, and you're willing to run real payroll and keep your salary defensible, the S-corp election usually makes sense. Run your actual numbers, including the New York City piece if it applies to you, before deciding. A structure that saves one business owner thousands can cost a different one money if the math isn't there yet.
