Royalties: Schedule C or Schedule E? Your 1099 Doesn't Decide That

You wrote the book, the song, or shot the photo that got licensed into a national campaign, or maybe your latest footage got featured in a documentary. Fast forward a few months in January, and a 1099-MISC shows up with a number in Box 2, labeled Royalties. Your tax software sees the word "royalties," drops the income on Schedule E next to rental properties, and the self-employment tax line stays at zero.

While it may feel like a win on the surface, the reality is something much different.

Published November 11th, 2026 | By Stephen Daperis, CPA

The pitch: royalties are passive income

The logic goes like this: royalties are money that shows up while you sleep, so that means its passive right? After all, page 1 of Schedule E literally says royalties, so how difficult can this really be?

Well, with taxes, nothing is ever as it seems. There are different types of royalties and the treatment comes down to the specifics.

The rule: what you do decides the schedule

The Schedule E instructions are direct about this: royalties go on Schedule E, except when you're in business as a self-employed writer, inventor, artist, or similar. According to IRS Publication 525, if you're self-employed, then they belong on Schedule C.

In plain English: if creating the work is your trade, the royalties are business income from that trade. They're no different from a fee a client paid you, they just arrived later, through a publisher, label, or licensing agency instead of an invoice.

Schedule E is for royalties you receive without being in the business of producing the work. Some examples are rights you inherited from a parent who was a songwriter, a royalty stream you bought as an investment or mineral royalties from land you own.

The box on the 1099 tells you what the payer called the payment. It doesn't tell you how you're taxed on it. The publisher filling out Box 2 has no idea whether you're a full-time illustrator, a working musician, a video producer, or someone who inherited a catalog.

For a musician, Box 2 might be performing-rights income from a publisher's check, or a label statement. For a video producer, it might be a stock footage library or a distributor paying you for a film you made. If you made it and it's your trade, it's Schedule C either way.

What getting it wrong actually costs

Self-employment tax you were supposed to pay. Schedule C income is subject to self-employment tax: 15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base ($184,500 for 2026), 2.9% Medicare on everything above it, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax once you pass $200,000 ($250,000 married filing jointly).

Report $60,000 of your own book royalties on Schedule E and you've skipped roughly $8,500 of SE tax. That isn't a savings. It's a balance due, with interest and penalties attached, waiting to be found when the IRS reviews your return.

Retirement contributions you're not allowed to make. Don't sell your future thinking about today. A solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA is funded from net earnings from self-employment. Royalty income on Schedule E isn't earned income, so it can't support a contribution. File your royalties on E and you've quietly shut off the single best tax shelter a self-employed creative has.

Underpaying SE tax and losing a five-figure retirement deduction in the same move is the worst of both worlds.

"But it's platform money, not royalties"

Same answer, different costume.

Whether you're paid through a distributor, YouTube AdSense, Patreon, Substack, stock photo licensing, stock footage licensing, Twitch subs (whatever really), the tax treatment is unchanged. This is true whether these land on a 1099-MISC, a 1099-NEC, or a 1099-K.

If you're actively making the content, the payouts are income from your trade and they should be reported on Schedule C.

The gray zone: royalties after you stop creating

What about the novelist who stopped writing ten years ago and still gets checks? How about the session musician who moved into teaching?

This is the one spot where the answer can go either way, and it turns on whether you're still in the trade. Royalties from work you created can remain self-employment income years later if you're still in that line of business. If you've genuinely left it, there's a real case for Schedule E. That's a facts-and-documentation question, and it deserves an actual analysis rather than whatever the software defaults to.

Advances aren't loans (for tax purposes)

An advance against royalties is taxable in the year you receive it. Nearly every individual creator is on the cash basis, so it doesn't matter that you haven't earned out. The check cleared, you control the money, and it's income now.

If something goes wrong later, say the manuscript is never delivered and you pay the advance back, you get a deduction in the year you repay. If the repayment is more than $3,000, Section 1341 (the claim of right rule) lets you either take the deduction or claim a credit for the tax you paid on that money in the earlier year, whichever saves you more. Fortunately, the tax code won't make you lose twice on the same dollar.

The real planning point: a large advance landing in one year with nothing set aside is the most common way you end up with an underpayment penalty. Size your next quarterly estimate the week the check clears.

Selling the rights: ordinary income, with one big exception

Sometimes the move isn't collecting royalties. It's selling the catalog.

A copyright you created yourself generally isn't a capital asset. Section 1221(a)(3) specifically excludes it. Sell the rights to your novel, your photo archive, or your footage library and the gain is ordinary income, taxed at the same rates as wages.

Unless you're a songwriter. Songwriters get an exception. Section 1221(b)(3) lets you elect to treat the sale of musical compositions, or copyrights in musical works, that you created as the sale of a capital asset. That's the difference between ordinary rates as high as 37% and long-term capital gains rates that top out at 20%. It's an election, not automatic, so it has to be made on the return for the year of the sale. Sorry everyone else, there is no equivalent for you, but maybe your musician friends can pick up the tab next time you're out.

The verdict

If you made it, and making it is what you do, your royalties go on Schedule C. Pay the SE tax, deduct your real business costs against the income, and use those earnings to fund a solo 401(k) or SEP. Schedule E is for royalties you own, not royalties you earn.

If you've been filing on Schedule E as an active creator, the fix is usually an amended return, not a panic. Better you catch it than an examiner does.

Common questions

Do royalties on a 1099-MISC automatically go on Schedule E?

No. Box 2 tells you what the payer called the payment. If you're in business as a writer, musician, artist, or other creator, royalties from your own work go on Schedule C.

Are book and music royalties subject to self-employment tax?

Yes, when you're in the business of creating the work. Royalties reported on Schedule C are subject to SE tax like any other self-employment income.

I inherited royalties from a parent's songs. Where do those go?

Schedule E. You didn't create the work and you aren't in that trade, so the royalties are investment-type income with no self-employment tax.

Can I fund a solo 401(k) with royalty income?

Only if the royalties are self-employment income on Schedule C. Royalties on Schedule E don't count as earned income for retirement contributions.

Is a book advance taxable before I earn out?

Yes. For a cash-basis creator, an advance is income when received. If you later repay it, you deduct the repayment, and repayments over $3,000 qualify for the claim of right rule in Section 1341.

I'm a video producer. Is stock footage income "royalties"?

Whatever the payer calls it, it's income from your trade. If you shot the footage and producing video is your business, license fees and royalties from a stock library go on Schedule C and are subject to self-employment tax.
