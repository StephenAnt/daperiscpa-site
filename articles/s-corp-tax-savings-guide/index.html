The S Corp Tax Savings Guide: How the Numbers Actually Work

Every S-corp pitch boils down to one sentence: split your income into salary and distributions, and only the salary part pays self-employment tax. That's true. It's also not the whole story, and the missing pieces are usually where people get burned.

Here's how it actually works, with the timing and trade-offs that decide whether this saves you real money or just adds complexity.

Published August 25th, 2026 | By Stephen Daperis, CPA

A Simple Example

Say a business makes $150,000 in profit for its owner (a round number, just for illustration). As a sole proprietor or a default LLC, all $150,000 gets hit with self-employment tax, 15.3% for Social Security and Medicare, on top of regular income tax. There's no way to carve any of it out.

Elect S-corp treatment, and that same $150,000 splits. Say the owner sets a reasonable salary of $80,000, run through payroll, taxed at the full 15.3% either way. The remaining $70,000 comes out as a distribution and skips that 15.3% completely. It's still taxed as regular income, just without the extra hit.

The savings in this example come entirely from that $70,000 dodging the 15.3% it would've paid as a sole proprietor. That's the whole point of the S-corp election. Everything else here is about what limits it or costs against it.

Your Salary Has to Be Real

You can't just pick a number that saves you the most tax. Your salary needs to reflect what someone else would actually get paid to do your job in your market. The IRS has gone after S-corp owners who paid themselves too little and taken large distributions instead, sometimes reclassifying the whole thing as wages after the fact, with back taxes and penalties on top. Base your number on what the role actually pays, and keep the records to back it up.

Paying yourself too much is its own mistake. Every extra dollar above a defensible number costs you payroll tax for nothing, and it can shrink a separate deduction covered below.

The Cost Nobody Mentions

Running an S corp means running actual payroll: registering as an employer, handling payroll taxes on schedule, filing payroll returns every quarter, issuing a W-2, on top of a separate corporate tax return. None of that is optional once you elect S-corp treatment, and none of it is free. At lower profit levels, this cost can eat up most or all of the tax savings. That's why this election tends to make sense above a certain profit level and not below it. Measure your savings against what it actually costs to run this properly, not against the best-case number.

The Deadline Is Real

To have S-corp treatment start this year, an existing business generally needs to file the election, Form 2553, by March 15. Miss it, and you're stuck paying self-employment tax on everything for another whole year. A brand-new business gets a bit more time, generally about two and a half months from when it's formed. This deadline catches people because the S-corp conversation usually happens in the spring at tax time, months after the window already closed. If you're even considering this, put the date on the calendar now.

The Deduction People Forget: QBI

There's a second piece worth knowing about. The Qualified Business Income deduction gets limited at higher income levels based on how much W-2 wages the business pays. A sole proprietor with no wages can lose the whole deduction once income crosses a certain point. An S-corp owner, because they're already paying themselves a salary, often keeps some or all of that deduction at the same income level. This can matter just as much as the self-employment tax savings for a high earner, and it rarely comes up in the conversation.

Putting It Together

The S-corp election saves real money, but only on the part of your profit above a defensible salary, only after you account for the added cost of payroll and filing, and only if you actually file the election on time. Run your own numbers, not the example above, before you decide, and if it's close, check again as your profit changes instead of deciding once and forgetting about it.
