Quarterly Estimated Taxes for Self-Employed New Yorkers

If nobody's withholding tax from your income, the IRS still expects to be paid four times a year, not once in April. For 2026, the federal due dates are April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15, 2027. Miss one and you're not just late, you're accruing a penalty from that due date forward, even if you pay everything in full by April.

Published August 3rd, 2026 | By Stephen Daperis, CPA

Who Actually Has to Pay

The federal trigger is $1,000. If you expect to owe $1,000 or more in federal tax for the year after subtracting withholding and credits, you're expected to make estimated payments. Freelancers, 1099 contractors, LLC owners taking draws, and anyone with meaningful side income without withholding all fall into this bucket.

New York has its own trigger. Estimated payments are generally required at the state level if you expect to owe more than $300 in New York tax for the year. New York's due dates follow the same federal schedule.

NYC residents pay through the same filing, not a separate form. New York City personal income tax gets calculated and paid alongside your state estimated payments, not through an entirely separate quarterly process.

How Much to Actually Set Aside

Self-employment tax is the number people forget

Income tax isn't the only thing hitting your quarterly bill. Self-employment tax, 15.3% covering Social Security and Medicare, applies on top of it for anyone who isn't running payroll through an S-Corp election. A lot of first-year freelancers calculate income tax, feel fine about the number, and get blindsided because they never accounted for SE tax at all.

The safe harbor rule is your friend

You generally avoid an underpayment penalty if you pay at least 90% of what you'll owe for the current year, or 100% of what you owed last year (110% if last year's income was high enough to trigger the higher threshold). Using last year's total tax bill, divided by four, is a simple and defensible way to set your quarterly number if this year's income is roughly similar.

Missing a payment isn't the end of the world, but it isn't free either

If you miss a quarter, pay it as soon as you can. The penalty is calculated using the IRS short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, running roughly 7-8% annualized in 2026, and it accrues from the missed due date until you pay or file your return, whichever comes first. A late Q1 payment costs more in penalty than a late Q4 payment simply because it sits unpaid longer.

What About Filing an Extension?

An extension buys you time to file, not time to pay

Form 4868 pushes your filing deadline out to October, but it does not move your payment deadline. Whatever you owe is still due on the original April date. File an extension and pay nothing, and you're accruing interest and a failure-to-pay penalty on the unpaid balance starting the day after the original deadline, extension or not. Filing an extension does not increase your odds of being audited.

It does not raise your audit odds

This is the myth that keeps people from filing one even when they genuinely need the extra time. The IRS selects returns for audit based on the information on the return itself, not on whether it arrived in April or October. A complete, accurate return filed in October carries no more audit risk than the same return filed in April. If anything, a rushed return filed to hit an arbitrary deadline, with numbers you didn't have time to verify, is the bigger risk.

File it if you need it

If your quarterly payments have kept you roughly current, an extension is a low-stakes move that buys breathing room to file an accurate return instead of a rushed one. Just pay your best estimate of what you owe by the original deadline first.

Keep It Simple / Key Takeaway 🍕

Set aside roughly 25-30% of every payment you receive into a separate account the moment it lands, before you touch it for anything else. When the quarterly deadlines roll around, you can easily make your payments online through IRS Direct Pay for your federal taxes and the NY State Department of Taxation & Finance for your state and city taxes. It's not an exact science, but it beats discovering in April that the number you owe and the number you have don't match. And remember, if you need more time to file, take it. It's not a red flag.
