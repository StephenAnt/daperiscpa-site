Real Estate Professional Status: What You Need To Know

Real estate professional status is the one designation that lets a serious investor deduct rental losses against W-2 income and business profit without the passive-activity cap that limits most landlords to a $25,000 allowance, though very large losses can still run into a separate limit under the excess business loss rules. The mistake people make is thinking 750 hours is the whole requirement. It's the first of two gates, and clearing the first one doesn't automatically clear the second.

Published July 31st, 2026 | By Stephen Daperis, CPA

Gate One: Qualifying as a Real Estate Professional

To be treated as a real estate professional under the tax code, you have to pass two tests in the same year, not one:

The 750-hour test. You need more than 750 hours of work during the year in real property trades or businesses, roughly 14-15 hours a week if spread evenly, though it doesn't need to be spread evenly at all.

The more-than-half test. Those 750-plus hours also have to represent more than half of all the personal service hours you worked in any trade or business that year, including your regular job. This is the test that quietly disqualifies most people with a demanding W-2 career. If you worked 2,000 hours at your day job, you'd need more than 2,000 hours in real estate to clear this test, not just 751.

What actually counts. Development, construction, acquisition, conversion, rental operation, property management, and brokerage all count. Passive research, reading market reports, and general financial planning don't. And if you're someone else's employee, those hours only count toward REPS if you own more than 5% of that employer.

The spouse rule that trips people up

If you're married filing jointly, only one spouse needs to clear both tests to qualify for REPS. The two of you cannot combine hours to get one spouse across the 750-hour line. Each spouse's qualification is measured individually.

Got a short-term rental? Here's the exception

Do you own an Airbnb or Vrbo? Under Treasury Regulation §1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A), if your average guest stay is 7 days or less, the IRS generally doesn't treat it as a "rental activity" in the first place, which means you skip the passive-by-default rental rules entirely and go straight to the material participation test, no Real Estate Professional Status required.

Clearing the 7-day average only removes the automatic passive label. You still have to independently clear material participation on that property, and one long booking can pull your annual average over the line and drop you right back into rental-activity treatment for the year.

2. Gate Two: Material Participation in Each Rental

REPS status alone doesn't make your losses deductible

Qualifying as a real estate professional only changes how your rental activities are classified. It doesn't automatically make the losses usable. You still have to separately materially participate in each rental property (or elect to group all your rental interests together and treat them as one combined activity) before those losses stop being passive.

The tests that actually apply to a rental

The IRS has several ways to prove material participation in a given activity. For rental real estate, the two that come up constantly are:

More than 500 hours in that specific activity during the year.

More than 100 hours, and at least as much as anyone else involved, including a property manager or contractor.

Here's where spousal hours actually do combine

Unlike the REPS qualification test itself, material participation in a specific rental activity does let you count both spouses' hours together. A spouse who doesn't personally clear the REPS threshold can still contribute hours toward material participation on a jointly owned property, once the other spouse has already qualified for REPS status.

Grouping saves you from doing this property by property

If you own several rental properties, proving material participation separately for each one gets unwieldy fast. Making a formal election under Section 469(c)(7)(A) lets you treat all your rental interests as one combined activity, so your hours across the whole portfolio count toward a single material participation test instead of needing to individually clear the bar on every property.

Keep It Simple / Key Takeaway 🍕

Think of REPS as unlocking the door and material participation as actually walking through it. Hit 750 hours without also materially participating in your rentals, and you've done the harder half of the work for nothing. Contemporaneous time logs matter for both tests, because the IRS scrutinizes this status closely, and reconstructed hours after the fact carry a lot less weight than a log kept in real time.
