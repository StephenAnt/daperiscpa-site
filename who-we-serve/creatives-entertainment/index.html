Tax Planning for Creatives & Entertainment Professionals

Creative income doesn't arrive like a paycheck. It shows up as client invoices, platform payouts, royalties, advances, and union W-2s, sometimes all in the same year, and each one is taxed differently. Actors, musicians, writers, video producers, and other creatives end up overpaying not because they're careless, but because generic tax prep treats every one of those payments the same way.

Where Creatives Actually Overpay

If your income comes from more than one direction, this is probably familiar.

Royalties filed on the wrong schedule. Your 1099 says "royalties," so the software puts them on Schedule E. If you created the work and it's your trade, they belong on Schedule C. Getting that wrong means unpaid self-employment tax now, and no solo 401(k) or SEP contributions from that income.

W-2 gigs with costs you can't deduct. Agent and manager commissions, union dues, coaching, instruments, your own camera gear, travel to the job. If a production pays you as an employee, none of it is deductible on your federal return, and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act made that permanent.

A loan-out built from a Los Angeles template. New York City doesn't recognize S corporations. Your loan-out pays the city's 8.85% General Corporation Tax on its profit, which changes the math on how much to take as salary versus distributions. Different states and cities have different rules when it comes to taxes.

Income that lands all at once. A big advance or a heavy project year with nothing set aside leads straight to an underpayment penalty. Creatives don't get income averaging (that's reserved for farmers and fishermen), so the only fix is planning before the money hits.

Freelance income without a business behind it. No separate account, no estimates, no retirement plan, and a first-time surprise from New York City's Unincorporated Business Tax once your income grows.

Common Questions from Creatives

My royalties came on a 1099-MISC. Do they go on Schedule E?

Only if you're not in the business of creating the work, for example royalties you inherited or bought. If you made it and it's your trade, royalties go on Schedule C. We break down why the form doesn't decide it.

Should I have a loan-out company?

If most of your income is W-2 production, session, or touring work and commissions and gear are a big share of your gross, it's worth modeling. In New York City, the city's 8.85% corporate tax changes the economics, so the decision should come from your numbers, not a template. Read more in our loan-out breakdown.

Can I deduct my agent's commission?

If you're paid on a W-2, generally no. Unreimbursed employee expenses are permanently non-deductible after the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. If you're self-employed or paid through a loan-out, commissions are an ordinary business expense.

I get a kit fee for my gear. How is that reported?

A kit fee is what a production pays you for gear you bring to the job, and how it's reported depends on how you're paid. If you're a 1099 contractor, it's simply part of your Schedule C receipts, with your gear costs deducted against it. If you're on payroll and the kit fee is its own line, it's usually reported as other income on Schedule 1, with no self-employment tax and deductions limited to that income. If you rent your gear out regularly to several productions or advertise it for rent, it's reported on Schedule C, with depreciation and other deductions. Either way, check your pay stub or 1099 before filing, because productions don't all handle kit fees the same way, and some pay them as wages.

I got a big advance this year. What should I do?

Treat it as income now, because it is. An advance is taxable when you receive it, even before you earn out. Make an estimated payment for that quarter so the advance doesn't turn into an underpayment penalty in April.

When should a freelancer elect S-corp status?

When your profit is steady and high enough that the payroll tax savings beat the added costs, which in New York City include the General Corporation Tax. We model it with your actual numbers before you file anything.
